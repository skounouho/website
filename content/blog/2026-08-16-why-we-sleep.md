## Summary

Neuroscientist Matthew Walker argues that sleep isn't simply idle time: it's a state of active maintenance work for the brain and body. While a few of the claims in the book are a bit hyperbolic, the crux of the book is well supported by research: getting a full night of sleep, over many nights, has a myriad of health benefits. And getting too little sleep, even over a short period of time, has significant and compounding consequences for health, performance, and memory.

## What is sleep?

Sleep alternates between two states in cycles of roughly 90 minutes.

**Non-REM** is the deep, quiet stage during which brain activity slows in long rolling waves (starting from behind your forehead, ending at the back of your head). This is when the day's memories get moved into long-term storage and the brain flushes out metabolic waste.

Rapid eye movement, or **REM**, is the opposite: the brain lights up to waking levels and the body is completely paralyzed. This is where dreaming happens, emotional experiences are processed, and disparate ideas recombine into concepts.

The mix shifts across the night: Non-REM dominates the earlier hours, REM the later ones.

## Myths

### Sleep is a lifestyle issue, not a health one

This is the fundamental myth the rest of the book exists to dismantle, and the evidence spans nearly every system.

- **Cardiovascular.** Heart attacks rise about 24% on the Monday after the spring daylight savings change and fall about 21% after the autumn one as hundreds of millions of people gain or lose a single hour on the same night. Many key processes, like clearing arterial waste and buildup, occur during sleep. Moreover, sleep deprivation stresses the body and raises blood pressure.
- **Neurological.** More than 60% of Alzheimer's patients had at least one clinical sleep disorder before being diagnosed with the disease. Alzheimer's is closely related to the build up of waste products in the brain. Because the waste clearing happens in deep sleep, poor sleep allows accumulation of this waste. Ronald Reagan and Margaret Thatcher are two famous examples of people who touted their short sleep schedules and later developed Alzheimers.
- **Immune.** Cutting a single night of sleep to 4 hours leads to a drop of 70% in the activity of natural killer T-cells, the immune cells that destroy tumor and virus-infected cells. Bad sleep also makes it harder for your body to create antibodies to flu shots. 
- **Metabolic.** Short sleep makes you feel hungrier and reduces your ability to feel full by impacting the hormones your body uses to communicate these signals. Moreover, a week of sleep deprivation makes healthy adults pre-diabetic in their blood sugar response.

Obviously prioritizing sleep, like all other aspects of health, is not for everyone. But Walker wants us to be aware of the damage that sleep deprivation does to our bodies and minds.

### Sleep resets every day, and people know when they slept well

In short: it doesn't, and the deficits stack instead. In one study, researchers put 48 adults on fixed sleep schedules of 8, 6, 4, or 2 hours a night for 2 weeks, measuring their reaction times throughout. After 10 nights at 6 hours, that group performed as badly as someone who'd been awake for a full 24 hours straight. The 4-hour group hit the same point in about 6 nights. Even worse, the sleep-deprived participants rated their own alertness as only slightly worse than normal, while their actual performance kept falling. After a while, we get used to sleep deprivation and stop noticing that we are tired and performing poorly.

### 6 hours is enough

6 hours is roughly what adults actually get on average, but it is not optimal. Essentially nobody operates on 6 hours with no measurable cost. There are certain people with genetics that allow for short sleep, but you are more likely to die in a shark attack than be one of these people. Often, older adults are told their sleep needs decline with age. It doesn't. What declines is the ability to *generate* sleep, especially deep non-REM, and that decline isn't a harmless feature of aging. It is causally linked with the memory loss we've learned to expect in old age. The less you sleep, the less you remember.

### Sleep is nothing happening

Most people think that the brain powers down to rest. But measure brain activity at night and the opposite shows up: during REM, brain activity matches or exceeds waking levels. Non-REM does the filing of memories and the cleaning. REM consolidates emotional experiences and connects abstract ideas and concepts. The body at-large cleans accumulated waste and stress and revitalizes immune pathways. We mistake our lack of consciousness for a lack of activity, and this could not be further from the truth.

### A nap can make up for it

Naps are genuinely useful, but as an addition to a full night rather than a patch on a short one. The reason is structural. Because REM is stacked into the final hours of sleep, cutting a night from 8 hours to 6 doesn't shave a quarter off each stage evenly: it takes most of its cut out of REM. Trimming those last 2 hours costs you the majority of your REM sleep for that night. In the long term, this reduces our ability to process stress and think creatively. Walker cites research that suggests that early start times for adolescents and teens can be linked with rising ADHD, anxiety, and depression: attention, stress, and emotional regulation are all reliant on REM sleep.

### Alcohol helps you sleep

Alcohol is a sedative, and sedation isn't sleep. It fragments the night into brief awakenings the sleeper doesn't remember, and it's particularly good at suppressing REM. In one experiment, students learned assigned material and were then split into 3 groups: one slept normally, one drank on the first night after learning, and one drank on the third night. Both drinking groups lost roughly half the memory benefit, including the group whose drinking came 2 days later.

### Only outdoor light impacts sleep cycles

The eye reports to the body's internal clock, and it's disproportionately sensitive to the blue wavelengths that LED screens emit. In one study, participants read on an iPad for 4 hours before bed across several nights, then did the same with printed books. The tablet nights pushed the start of their sleep cycle about 3 hours later, cut REM sleep, and left them measurably groggier the next morning. In effect, reading on a screen before bed is equivalent to the jet lag of traveling from New York to San Francisco. Even ordinary room lighting does a milder version of the same thing.

### Sleeping pills help you sleep

Sleeping pills, like alcohol, produce sedation. They dose the brain in drugs that reduce activity. In a seminal wide-scale study, researchers found people prescribed sleeping pills died at several times the rate of similar people who weren't. The results were purely correlation, not causation. But it's been replicated enough to be taken seriously. Meanwhile the alternative works: cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) matches or beats the drug interventions, keeps working after treatment ends, and has been the recommended first treatment since 2016.

To give an example of CBT-I, sometimes patients will start by restricting their sleep to, say 5 hours per night. As long as they avoid all naps, the sleep deprivation builds sleep pressure, making it easier to fall asleep the next night. Continue this for a few weeks, and the patient has more confidence in their ability to generate sleep. At this point, the restriction is *gradually* lifted until the patient is easily producing 8 hours a night.
